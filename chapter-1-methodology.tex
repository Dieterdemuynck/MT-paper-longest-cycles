\subsection{Intersection of cycles}

One of the aims of this thesis is to create an algorithm that can determine if a graph has circumference 11, and has no vertex meeting every 11-cycle for RQ\ref{rq1}. Finding the longest cycle in a general graph, known as longest cycle problem, ``is well known as NP-complete.''~\cite{article:longest-cycle-np}, meaning there is no known polynomial algorithm for solving it and remains computationally expensive. In this thesis, the approach used is to enumerate all cycles, which lets us determine if a cycle of length 11 exists and no larger. This enumeration also provides an easy method for computing the required intersection, as we just take the intersection of all found 11-cycles. Thus, we need an algorithm for enumerating all cycles efficiently.

% --- COMMENT: KEEP IN METHODOLOGY. The complexity result could potentially be mentioned briefly in Background as theoretical motivation, but this paragraph is mainly motivating your chosen algorithmic approach and should remain here. ---

\subsubsection{Enumeration of all cycles once}

The simplest approach to this problem is a depth-first search on the vertices. The algorithm goes as follows: take a starting vertex and add it to the current path. Then, repeat the following steps until the path is empty. Take an unvisited neighbor of the last vertex in the path, and consider that vertex as a candidate next vertex. If this candidate is the original starting vertex (and there are at least three vertices in the path), then we have found a cycle, and the vertices of the cycle are the visited vertices together with the starting vertex. The length of this cycle equal to the amount of vertices in the path. If the last vertex has no more unvisited edges, we can consider this vertex spent and pop it from the list of visited vertices, which also requires us to set its edges as unvisited again.

% --- COMMENT: KEEP IN METHODOLOGY. This is the algorithmic procedure. It may need editing for clarity/correctness later, but that is separate from the chapter relocation. ---

This algorithm by itself is not yet efficient, as during enumeration, each cycle is found once for every vertex it contains, times two. This because the algorithm takes every vertex of the cycle as a starting vertex, and discovers the cycle each time twice, in the two directions it can travel down the cycle. We can reduce this number by using a useful property of the way vertices of graphs are often represented in computer programs. In this thesis' source code, vertices are represented as integers, from 0 to $n-1$ with $n$ equal to the order of the graph. This provides us with the useful property that the vertices are \textit{ordered}.

% --- COMMENT: KEEP IN METHODOLOGY. This explains an implementation-level optimization. The general mathematical notion of a total ordering could be introduced in Background only if needed, but the use of the ordering in your algorithm belongs here. ---

The ordering of the vertices allows us to consider the starting vertex as the \textit{minimal} vertex, where we only allow new vertices to be candidates if they are higher in order than this starting vertex. The algorithm would then run starting from every This greatly reduces the number of times each cycle is enumerated, from twice for every vertex in both directions, to only twice from the minimal vertex of the cycle. Furthermore, we can also use the ordering of the vertices to force the cycle to only be enumerated in one of the two directions. This by requiring that the cycle's first and last vertex (other than the starting vertex) must be in, for example, ascending order, and ignoring found cycles which are in descending order.

\subsubsection{Halting cycle enumeration early}

This algorithm allows us to enumerate all cycles of the graph exactly once, under the assumption the graph is connected. We can get an exact value of the circumference of the graph by setting the circumference equal to 0 at the start of the enumeration and, for each found cycle, taking the maximum of the current believed circumference and the length of the cycle. Note that for RQ\ref{rq1} we are only interested in the graphs with circumference 11. As a result, we can halt enumeration of the cycles early when a graph is found to have a cycle that is larger than 11, as said graph is no longer relevant for RQ\ref{rq1}.

% --- COMMENT: KEEP IN METHODOLOGY. This is a concrete optimization based on the research question. ---

Additionally, cycles that are smaller than 11 are also uninteresting. If no 11-cycle or larger is found, then the circumference of the graph is lower than 11, and again not relevant for RQ\ref{rq1}. Therefore, we can safely reduce the number of starting vertices, by skipping the 10 highest ordered nodes, as running DFS with these as minimal vertices can only result in cycles of length 10 or smaller.

% --- COMMENT: KEEP IN METHODOLOGY. This is a specific algorithmic optimization. It may benefit from a more explicit proof/explanation, but does not need to move to Background. ---

During enumeration, we must also compute the intersection of all 11-cycles. Set the initial intersection $I$ equal to all vertices of the graph $V(G)$ at the start of enumeration. Whenever an 11-cycle is enumerated, set $I$ equal to the intersection of $I$ and the vertices of the 11-cycle. The result at the end of the enumeration will be the intersection of all 11-cycles if there are any, or all vertices otherwise. If there are no 11-cycles, then the computed circumference is smaller than 11 (or larger if computation halted early due to a larger cycle), and therefore irrelevant for RQ\ref{rq1}.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{\textsc{FindIntersectionK}$(G,k,\prec)$}
\label{alg:find-k-intersection}

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Graph $G$, integer $|V(G)|>k>2$, and a total ordering on $V$
\Ensure intersection of all $k$-cycles $\textit{intersection}$ and circumference $c(G)=k$

\State $\textit{intersection} \gets V(G)$
\ForAll{$s \in V(G)$ ascending}
\State $\textit{path} \gets [s]$
\While{$\textit{path}$ is not empty}
    \State $u \gets$ the last vertex in $\textit{path}$
    \If{no unvisited neighbor of $u$}
        \State remove $u$ from $\textit{path}$
        \Comment Including its visited neighbors
        \State \textbf{continue}
    \EndIf
    \State {$v \gets$ an unvisited vertex, and unvisited neighbor of $u$}
    \State mark $v$ as visited neighbor of $u$
    \If{$v=s$}
        \State $\ell \gets |\textit{path}|$
        \If{$\ell > k$}
            \State \Return $\text{LARGER\_THAN\_K}$
        \EndIf
        \If{$\ell = k$}
            \State $\textit{intersection} \gets
            \textit{intersection} \cap \textit{path}$
        \EndIf
        \State \textbf{continue}
    \EndIf
    \If{$s \prec v$}
        \State add $v$ to $\textit{path}$
    \EndIf
\EndWhile
\EndFor

\If{$\textit{intersection} = V(G)$}
    \State \Return $\text{SMALLER\_THAN\_K}$
\EndIf
\State \Return $\text{EQUAL\_TO\_K}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This section introduces the algorithm for RQ\ref{rq2}. The approach is similar to RQ\ref{rq1}: we generate planar graphs with minimum degree 4, and check for each graph if in every vertex-deleted subgraph there exists a $k$-cycle, that there then is a cycle larger than $k$. Trivially, if a vertex-deleted subgraph of a graph $G$ has a $k$-cycle, then the graph $G$ itself also has a $k$-cycle. As a result, for a graph $G$ can only be a negative example of RQ\ref{rq2}, there must be a $k$-cycle in every vertex-deleted subgraph of $G$ and $G$ must also have circumference $k$. From this, we decided to focus our algorithm on finding negative examples, as we can assume all other graphs to be positive examples.
% --- COMMENT: KEEP IN METHODOLOGY. This explains the computational strategy for RQ2. However, the definitions of vertex-deleted subgraphs, circumference, and relevant graph properties should be available in Background. ---

\begin{theorem}
  \label{theorem:intersection-vertex-deletion}
  A graph $G$ has a $k$-cycle in every vertex-deleted subgraph, and no cycle larger than $k$ in $g$ if and only if the intersection of the vertices of all $k$-cycles in the graph is empty, and $G$ has circumference $k$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Assume $G$ has a $k$-cycle in every vertex-deleted subgraph, and no cycle larger than $k$ exists in $G$. $G$ must have at least one $k$-cycle, denoted $C$, and therefore must have circumference $k$. The intersection of all $k$-cycles must be at most the vertices $V(C) \subset V(G)$. Per the assumption, for every vertex $v_i \in V(G)$ there must be a $k$-cycle $C_i'$ in $G$ avoiding the vertex $v_i$, including those vertices $v_i \in V(C)$. As a result, the intersection of all $k$-cycles is at most
  $$
  \bigcap_{i} C_i' \cap C = \emptyset
  $$
  and thus the intersection of all $k$-cycles must be empty.

  Now assume $G$ has circumference $k$ and the intersection of all $k$-cycles is empty. $G$ cannot be hamiltonian, as otherwise the intersection of the vertices of all $k$-cycles must be the vertices $V(G)$. $G$ must have at least one $k$-cycle $C$, which avoids all vertices $v_{\text{out}} \in V(G) \setminus V(C)$. Additionally, since the intersection of all $k$-cycles is empty, there must be a $k$-cycle for every vertex $v_{\text{in}} \in V(C)$ which avoids $v_{\text{in}}$. As a result, there exists a $k$-cycle avoiding every vertex $v \in V(C) \cup (V(G) \setminus V(C)) = V(G)$, and therefore every vertex-deleted subgraph of $G$ has a $k$-cycle.

  We conclude that for a graph $G$, $G$ has a $k$-cycle in every vertex-deleted subgraph and has circumference $k$ if and only if the intersection of the vertices of all $k$-cycles in the graph is empty.
\end{proof}

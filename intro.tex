Graph theory is an important field in computer sciences, capable of modeling a vast number of problems. Graphs are conceptually simple mathematical objects, which represent \textit{things} or nodes and connections between these nodes, and yet they are very important for many different applications. For example, a graph may represent a computer network, where the nodes are computers, and two computers are connected if there is a way for the computers to communicate directly with one another (either through a physical cable or wirelessly). It may also represent cities, whith connected cities having a road between them. In these problems, graph theory can help find the shortest path from one \textit{thing} to another, allowing one to compute, for example, the quickest route through what sequence of cities to get from city A to city B, using Dijkstra's shortest path algorithm~\cite{article:dijkstra}. Graphs can also represent more abstract concepts, such as people in a \textit{friends-with}-relationship, or even the different possible board states of a chess game, connecting states which can be reached in one move.

Cycles, a sequence of connected nodes which ends on the first node, are also very important concepts in graph theory. Take the concrete concept of a city and roads for example. A salesman trying to buy and sell wares in the different cities may want to know what the shortest route is that goes through every city once. This problem is known as the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP)~\cite{article:TSP}, and is a problem that is known to be NP-hard (i.e. computationally expensive) to solve quickly. Alternatively, there is also the question whether a given graph has a cycle going though every node just once, which is known as the Hamiltonian Cycle Problem~\cite{article:hamiltonian-planar}. Another important example is cycle-detection, which helps with solving, for example, deadlocks. If two or more processes are waiting on each other to finish something before continuing, they may all wait indefinitely. A cycle-detection algorithm can help find, and fix or prevent potential deadlocks~\cite{article:deadlocks}.

The study of cycles in graphs has expanded to more abstract mathematical problems. One may want to find out what nodes of a given graph they need to remove to also destroy all longest cycles. Building on top of that, van Aardt et al. attempted to answer the question as to what the upper bound is for the fraction of nodes of a graph need to be deleted to make sure there is a way to destroy all longest cycles in the graph. This has been studied in the article \textit{Destroying longest cycles in graphs and digraphs}~\cite{article:van-aardt} by van Aardt et al. In this article remained a few open questions, one of which was on the existence of graphs with, among others, the property that for some value $k$, all nodes of the graph avoided some longest cycle, each of which having $k$ nodes. This question was partially answered for some of the values $k$ in the paper \textit{On a question of van Aardt et al. on destroying all longest cycles}~\cite{article:zamfirescu} by Prof. Carol T. Zamfirescu. This last article also posed a new question, on a link between having a $k$-long cycle per node avoiding that node, and the existence of a longer cycle, within a specific class of graphs. 

This thesis aims to fully solve the question by van Aardt et al., and to provide new insights into the question posed by Zamfirescu, by exhaustively  generating a large number of graphs, and computationally checking these graphs' properties related to these questions. In Chapter~\ref{cha:background}, we define the relevant terminology, as well formally stating the questions this thesis aims to solve. This chapter also explains the important results from previous literature, and the use of graph generators, which exist, and for what purpose. In Chapter~\ref{cha:methodology}, we explain the generators and programs used, and the algorithms designed to solve the problem. This chapter also formalizes what processors these programs were run on. In Chapter~\ref{cha:results}, we show the computational results and the timings of the implemented algorithms and generators or generation setups, as well as the theoretical results found. Finally, we explain the conclusions gathered from the results and the future work that could build off of this thesis in Chapter~\ref{cha:conclusion}.

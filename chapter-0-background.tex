\chapter{Background}
\label{cha:background}

In this chapter, we define the terminology from graph theory that is relevant to and used in this thesis. We also show important results, observations and theorems from the literature, before continuing on to formalizing the two important research questions of this thesis. After this, we explain graph generation, giving some example generators of which some are used in this thesis.

\section{Definitions}

All the following definitions are based on the book \textit{Graph Theory} by Adrian Bondy and U.S.R.\ Murty~\cite{book:bondy-definitions}. We begin by defining a graph formally.

% Graph explanation
\begin{definition}[Graph]
  A \textbf{graph} $G$ is a mathematical structure represented by an ordered pair $(V(G), E(G))$ consisting of a set of \textbf{vertices} $V(G)$ (singular: vertex) and a (multi-)set of \textbf{edges} $E(G)$ which are unordered pairs of vertices\footnote{This definition is different from~\cite{book:bondy-definitions}, which defines edges as abstract objects with an \textit{incidence function} which maps each edge to an unordered pair of vertices.} $(v,w) \in V(G) \times V(G)$.
\end{definition}

Graphs are often represented graphically with circles representing vertices, and lines ending at circles to represent edges between two vertices, such as in Figure~\ref{fig:example-simple}. This graph is also \textbf{simple}, meaning that there are no edges $(v,v)$ and there is at most one edge between any two vertices. The graph in Figure~\ref{fig:example-non-planar} is not simple. In the rest of this thesis, \emph{graph} is used to refer to simple graphs. We say that an edge $(u, v)$ \textbf{joins} the two vertices $u$ and $v$, and is \textbf{incident} on each of the vertices $u$ and $v$. Lastly, we say two vertices are \textbf{adjacent} if there is an edge which joins them. The number of vertices of a graph is an important property commonly used to characterize its size. The following definition formalizes this notion.

\begin{definition}[Order]
  The \textbf{order} of a graph $G$, denoted $n(G)$, is the number of vertices $|V(G)|$.
\end{definition}

There are multiple data structures to represent graphs. Two examples of these are the \textbf{adjacency list} and the \textbf{adjacency matrix}. In an adjacency list, one keeps a list of adjacent vertices for every vertex in the graph. These lists are then stored in another list. An adjacency matrix of a graph $G$ is an $n(G) \times n(G)$ matrix $A_G := (a_{uv})$, where $a_{uv} \in \mathbb{N}$ (with $0 \in \mathbb{N}$) represents the number of edges between the vertices $u$ and $v$. For simple graphs, this is $1$ if there is an edge, and $0$ if not. Figure~\ref{fig:adjacency} shows an adjacency list and an adjacency matrix for the graph in Figure~\ref{fig:example-simple}.

In this thesis, we may refer to the number of edges incident with a vertex, as well as the minimum and maximum such numbers in a graph. These notions are defined as follows.

\begin{definition}[Degree]
  In a simple graph, the \textbf{degree} of a vertex $v$, denoted $\text{deg}(v)$ is the number of edges that are incident on the vertex. The \textbf{minimum degree} of a graph $G$ is the minimum degree of all its vertices, denoted $\delta(G)$. Analogously, we define the \textbf{maximum degree} denoted $\Delta(G)$ as the maximum of the degrees of all its vertices $V(G)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Planar graph]
  A graph is called \textbf{planar} if it can be drawn in the plane such that edges meet only at points corresponding to their common end, i.e. edges can be drawn such that they do not cross other edges or vertices.
\end{definition}

We call such a drawing a \textbf{planar embedding} of the graph. The graph in Figure~\ref{fig:example-simple} is planar, while that in Figure~\ref{fig:example-non-planar} is not.

\begin{definition}[Subgraph]
  A \textbf{subgraph} $F$ of a graph $G$ is a graph for which $V(F) \subseteq V(G)$ and $E(F) \subseteq E(G)$, and the edges $E(F)$ only contain vertices in $V(F)$.
\end{definition}

% Subgraph explanation
If the subgraph $F$ is the result of removing one vertex $v$ and the edges incident on $v$ from $G$ (notation: $G - v$), then we say $F$ is a\textbf{ vertex-deleted} subgraph of $G$. The graph in Figure~\ref{fig:example-simple} is a subgraph of the graph in Figure~\ref{fig:example-non-planar}.

\begin{definition}[Path]
  A \textbf{path} is a simple graph $P$ whose vertices $\{v_0,\ldots, v_{n-1}\} = V(P)$ can be arranged in a linear sequence $(v_0,\ldots,v_{n-1})$ such that two vertices are adjacent if and only if they are consecutive in the sequence. The \textbf{length} of a path is the number of edges it contains.
\end{definition}

This definition formalizes the problem from Chapter~\ref{cha:intro} of finding a sequence of cities to travel from city A to city B, as finding a path between a vertex $v_a$ to another vertex $v_b$ in the graph of cities $G_\textit{cities}$. The definition of a cycle is defined similarly.

\begin{definition}[Cycle]
  A \textbf{cycle} $C$ on three or more vertices is a simple graph whose vertices $\{v_0,\ldots, v_{n-1}\} = V(C)$ can be arranged in a cyclical sequence, such as $(v_0, \ldots, v_{n-1}, v_0)$, such that two vertices are adjacent if and only if they are consecutive in the sequence. The \textbf{length} of a cycle is the number of edges it contains.
\end{definition}

We denote a path or cycle of length $k$ as a \textbf{$k$-path} or \textbf{$k$-cycle} respectively. Examples of a 6-path and a 7-cycle are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cycle-and-path}. Observe that in a cycle, the number of edges is always equal to the number of vertices. We emphasize that a cycle or path does not take any vertex or edge more than once.

A central object of study in this thesis is the longest cycle of a graph. We define the term describing this property of a graph. 

\begin{definition}[Circumference]
  The \textbf{circumference} $c(G)$ of a graph $G$ containing at least one cycle is equal to the length of the longest cycle in the graph. We define the circumference of a cycle-free graph as $0$.
\end{definition}

Note that there may be multiple different longest cycles in any one graph. For example, Figure~\ref{fig:infinity-graph} shows a graph containing two longest cycles, each of length 4.

In this thesis, the vertices which two or more (sub-)graphs have in common is an important property. For that reason, we define the following ourselves.

\begin{definition}[Intersection]
  The \textbf{vertex-intersection}, from here on simply \textbf{intersection}, of two or more graphs $G_0,\ldots,G_{n-1}$ is the intersection of the vertex sets of all graphs $\bigcap_{i} V(G_i)$.
\end{definition}

We will mostly speak of the vertex intersection of $k$-cycles in a graph. For example, Figure~\ref{fig:infinity-graph} has a 4-cycle intersection equal to $\{v\}$. As a second example, take Figure~\ref{fig:petersen-graph}, a graph of order 10 known as the \textbf{Petersen graph}, which has a 9-cycle in every vertex-deleted subgraph. There is no cycle containing all vertices, and as such these 9-cycles are the longest cycles. Consequently, the intersection of all  longest cycles of this graph is empty.

Trivially, graphs which have a cycle through all vertices of the graph (such as in Figure~\ref{fig:cycle-subfigure}) have a longest cycle intersection equal to all vertices. Graphs which adhere to this property also have a name, defined as follows.

\begin{definition}[Hamiltonian graphs]
  If a graph $G$ has a cycle $C$ which contains every vertex in the graph $G$, then $C$ is called a \textbf{Hamilton} cycle of $G$, and $G$ is said to be \textbf{Hamiltonian}. A graph without a Hamilton cycle is called \textbf{non-Hamiltonian}.
\end{definition}

Since this thesis researches the intersection of longest cycles of graphs, Hamiltonian graphs are not interesting for the research questions this thesis aims to answer. There is, however, an interesting class of non-Hamiltonian graphs in which an interesting result was found in the context of this thesis.

\begin{definition}[Hypohamiltonian graphs]
  If a graph $G$ is not Hamiltonian, but every vertex-deleted subgraph $H$ of $G$ is, then $G$ is called \textbf{hypohamiltonian}.
\end{definition}

The smallest hypohamiltonian graph is the Petersen graph~\cite{article:van-aardt}, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:petersen-graph}. This graph is known to ``have many special properties''~\cite{book:bondy-definitions}, which are used to construct the interesting result in Section~\ref{sec:modified-petersen} found by C.\ T.\ Zamfirescu.

For some graphs, one can rearrange and rename the vertices of one graph (and update the respective edges) to get another graph, all without removing or adding any vertices or edges. These graphs are in some ways ``the same,'' and many properties such as the existence of cycles or paths (of a specific length) are the same for these graphs. We define this concept formally using the following definition.

\begin{definition}[Isomorphism]
  Two graphs $G$ and $H$ are \textbf{isomorphic} if there exist a bijection $\theta : V(G) \rightarrow V(H)$ such that for all vertices $v_0, v_1 \in V(G)$ that $(v_0,v_1) \in E(G)$ if and only if $(\theta(v_0),\theta(v_1)) \in E(H)$.
\end{definition}

The definition of a graph places no restrictions on which edges must exist. For example, a graph may consist of several isolated vertices or several disconnected parts. This motivates the following definition.

\begin{definition}[Connected]
  A graph is \textbf{connected} if for every partition of vertices into non-empty sets $X, Y$ there exists an edge with one vertex in $X$ and another in $Y$. A graph which is not connected is called \textbf{disconnected}.
\end{definition}

This definition is equivalent to defining that a graph is connected if there exists a path between every two distinct vertices $u$ and $v$ of the graph.

\begin{definition}[Vertex cut]
  Let $G$ be a connected graph. A vertex cut of $G$ is a set $S \subseteq V(G)$ such that $G - S$ is disconnected or consists of a single vertex.
\end{definition}

The notion of connectedness can be generalized further. One motivation for a more general notion of connectivity arises in, for example, communication networks. A network should ideally remain connected after the failure of one or more computers. This motivates the notion of $k$-connectivity, which measures the resilience of a graph against vertex deletions.

\begin{definition}[$k$-connected]
  A graph $G$ is \textbf{$k$-vertex-connected} (or simply $k$-connected) if all vertex cuts of $G$ have at least $k$ vertices.
\end{definition}

Consequently, deleting any number of vertices less than $k$ of a $k$-connected graph will guarantee the resulting graph is connected. By Menger's theorem~\cite{book:bondy-definitions}, this definition is equivalent to the following. A graph $G$ is $k$-connected if for every pair of distinct vertices $u,v \in V(G)$, there exist at least $k$ pairwise internally disjoint paths from $u$ to $v$, and when $G$ consists of only one vertex, it is considered to be 0-connected and 1-connected, but not $k$-connected for any $k > 1$. Two paths from $v_1$ to $v_2$ are internally (vertex-)disjoint if the vertex-intersection of said paths is equal to $\{v_1, v_2\}$. As a consequence of Menger's theorem, the definition of a 1-connected graph is equivalent to the definition of a connected graph.

\begin{definition}[Connectivity]
  The \textbf{connectivity} of a graph is the largest integer $k$ for which the graph is $k$-connected.
\end{definition}

The graph in Figure~\ref{fig:example-simple} is 3-connected, but not 4-connected, as removing all three vertices on any diagonal would result in a disconnected graph. This graph thus has connectivity 3. Similarly, every cycle has connectivity 2, and every path has connectivity 1, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:cycle-and-path}~\cite{book:bondy-definitions}.

\section{Background Literature}

\subsection{Longest Cycles and Hypohamiltonian Graphs}
\label{sec:thomassen-theorems}

Hypohamiltonian graphs have played an important role in the study of Hamilton cycles. In his study of hypohamiltonian graphs, Thomassen~\cite{article:thomassen} investigated several structural properties of this graph class.

Thomassen proves the following.
\begin{theorem}[\cite{article:thomassen}]
  \label{theorem:thomassen-planar-hypo-minimal}
  Every planar hypohamiltonian graph contains a vertex of degree $3$.
\end{theorem}
Consequently, no planar hypohamiltonian graph can have minimum degree $4$. Thomassen also considered the relationship between longest cycles of a graph, and he proved a conjecture of Tudor Zamfirescu using the following theorem.
\begin{theorem}[\cite{article:thomassen}]
  \label{theorem:thomassen-longest-intersect}
  Every graph on $n$ vertices contains a set of at most $n/3$ vertices that intersects every longest cycle.
\end{theorem}

One of the problems posed by Thomassen asks for which integers $k$ there exists a hypohamiltonian graph of connectivity (or minimum degree) $k$. He remarks that every known hypohamiltonian graph has connectivity and minimum degree $3$, making the case $k=4$ particularly interesting~\cite{article:thomassen}.

\subsection{Destroying longest cycles}

Van Aardt et al.\ continue the study of small vertex sets meeting all longest cycles, using the result by Thomassen, on the size of a set of vertices which intersects all longest cycles, as the starting point~\cite{article:van-aardt}. In their research, van Aardt et al.\ introduced the parameter $\alpha(k)$.

\begin{definition}[$\alpha(k)$~\cite{article:van-aardt}]
  For each $k \ge 3$, let $\alpha(k)$ be the smallest number such that every graph $G$ with circumference $k$ contains a set $A$ of $\lfloor \alpha(k)n(G)\rfloor$ vertices such that $G - A$ has no $k$-cycles.
\end{definition}

Informally, the parameter $\alpha(k)$ represents the fraction of vertices needed to ensure one can find a set $A$ using at most that many vertices of a graph $G$, to destroy all longest cycles of $G$, assuming those cycles have length $k$. Van Aardt et al.\ show this number must be at least $1/k$ by considering any number of disjoint cycles, as in Figure~\ref{fig:disjoint-cycles}, where the vertices marked red are a set $A$ destroying all (longest) cycles if deleted. Using Theorem~\ref{theorem:thomassen-longest-intersect}, van Aardt et al.\ show that $\alpha(k) \le 1/3$ for all $k$.

They showed that if every $2$-connected graph of circumference $k$ contains a vertex meeting every longest cycle, then $\alpha(k)=1/k$. Consequently, determining the existence of $2$-connected graphs whose longest cycles have empty intersection is essential for determining $\alpha(k)$.

Van Aardt et al.\ proved the existence or nonexistence of such graphs for all $k$ except for $k\in\{10,11,13,16\}$~\cite{article:van-aardt}. Zamfirescu~\cite{article:zamfirescu} subsequently constructed an infinite family of graphs answering this question for $k=10$, $13$ and $16$, leaving only the case $k=11$ unresolved. In the same paper, Zamfirescu also proved the existence of a graph with circumference $11$ and order $13$, in which all but one vertex avoids some $11$-cycle.

\subsection{Constructions by Zamfirescu}

\subsubsection{Solutions for $k \in \{10, 13, 16\}$}
\label{sec:tube-graph}

Zamfirescu showed~\cite{article:zamfirescu} that for every pair of integers $\ell \ge 2$ and $p \ge 4$, there exists a 2-connected graph $G$ of order $(\ell+1) p$ and circumference $(\ell+1) (p-1) + 1$ whose intersection of all longest cycles is empty. We repeat the construction of the graph $G$ used in the proof here. Note that in this thesis, we use a different convention for $\ell$ than in the paper~\cite{article:zamfirescu}.
Take two cycles $(a_0, a_1, ..., a_{p-1}, a_0)$ and $(b_0, b_1, ..., b_{p-1}, b_0)$, each of length $p$, and join the vertices $(a_i, b_i)$ for all $i$. Then, insert vertices on each edge $(a_i, b_i)$ until each becomes a path of length $\ell$ (i.e., has $\ell$ edges or $\ell+1$ vertices, including the vertices $a_i$ and $b_i$). Denote these paths from $a_i$ to $b_i$ as $H_i$. If $p$ is even, remove the edges $(a_{p-1},a_0)$ and $(b_{p-1},b_0)$, and replace them with the edges $(a_{p-1},b_0)$ and $(b_{p-1},a_0)$. We denote the graph constructed in this way as $G_{p,\ell}$. Zamfirescu has shown~\cite{article:zamfirescu} that the cycle $C$ with vertices
$$
\bigcup_{i \neq 0} V(H_i) \cup \{b_0\}
$$
and edges
$$
\bigcup_{i \neq 0} E(H_i) \;
  \cup {(a_j,a_{j+1})}_{j\ \text{odd}} \;
  \cup {(b_k,b_{k+1})}_{k>0\ \text{even}} \;
  \cup \begin{cases}
    \emptyset\ \text{if}\ p\ \text{odd} \\
    (a_{p-1},b_0)\ \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
$$
is a longest cycle of the graph $G$ avoiding all vertices $v \in V(H_0) \setminus \{b_0\}$, and that a similar cycle can be constructed for all other vertices. Figure~\ref{fig:tube-graph-odd} shows the graph $G_{5,2}$ alongside one longest cycle in the graph, and similarly Figure~\ref{fig:tube-graph-even} shows the graph $G_{4,3}$ alongside one longest cycle. Zamfirescu also notes that one can add any additional edges to each path $H_i$ internally (but not joining vertices from two different $H_i$) does not change the relevant properties of the graph~\cite{article:zamfirescu}.

\subsubsection{Single vertex intersection for $k=11$}
\label{sec:modified-petersen}

While a 2-connected graph of circumference 11 with no vertex meeting every 11-cycle is unknown, we do have an upper bound for the smallest amount of vertices in the intersection of all 11-cycle. In~\cite{article:zamfirescu}, Carol T. Zamfirescu constructs a 2-connected graph of circumference 11 in which all 11-cycles avoid all but one vertex. This graph exploits the properties of the Petersen graph, which is a hypohamiltonian graph of order 10 and thus circumference 9, by extending all three edges $(v,w_i)$ incident on one vertex $v$ with one extra vertex each, denoted $x_i$, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:petersen-divided}. This extends all cycles through this vertex $v$ by 2, creating a graph of circumference 11. We denote this graph by $P$. One can prove that as a result, this new graph of order 13 and circumference 11 has only one vertex $v$ meeting every 11-cycle~\cite{article:zamfirescu}.

\subsection{Exhaustive generation of planar graphs}

J.\ Goedgebeur, T.\ Gringore, and C.\ T.\ Zamfirescu~\cite{article:hamiltonian-planar} performed exhaustive com\-puter-assisted searches for small non-Hamiltonian planar graphs with minimum degree at least 4. Their work resulted in some key observations which could prove useful for this thesis.

\begin{observation}[\cite{article:hamiltonian-planar}]
  \label{obs:3con-hamiltonian}
  Every 3-connected planar graph with minimum degree at least 4 on at most 22 vertices is Hamiltonian.
\end{observation}

Consequently, any non-Hamiltonian planar graph with minimum degree at least 4 on at most 22 vertices must have connectivity at most 2.

\begin{observation}[\cite{article:hamiltonian-planar}]
  \label{obs:2con-n14}
  The smallest non-Hamiltonian 2-connected planar graphs with minimum degree at least four have 14 vertices, and exactly two such graphs exist. Both graphs have circumference 10.
\end{observation}

For this last observation, each of the non-Hamiltonian graphs up to 20 vertices were saved into a list. These graphs are reused in this thesis to reduce the computational effort required for the search related to Research Question~\ref{rq2}, which we formalize in the next section.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rq}

With this background, we can write down the open questions which this thesis aims to answer. The first research question of this thesis aims to provide an answer to the open question by van Aardt et al.\ for $k=11$~\cite{article:van-aardt}.

\begin{researchquestion}
\label{rq1}
Does there exist a 2-connected graph with circumference 11
that has no vertex meeting every 11-cycle?
\cite{article:van-aardt}
\end{researchquestion}

In the same article, van Aardt et al.\ state that there is no hypohamiltonian graph on 12 vertices, which requires a solution to RQ\ref{rq1} to have at least 13 vertices. Zamfirescu~\cite{article:zamfirescu} also asked the following more general question.

\begin{researchquestion}
\label{rq2}
For which values of $k$ is it true that if in a planar graph $G$ with minimum degree at least $4$ all vertex-deleted subgraphs contain a cycle of length $k$, then $G$ contains a cycle of length greater than $k$?
~\cite{article:zamfirescu}
\end{researchquestion}

Thomassen showed~\cite{article:thomassen} that the aforementioned statement is true for $k = n - 1$, where $n$ is the order of $G$. This is because no planar hypohamiltonian graph with a minimum degree of at least 4 can exist, using Theorem~\ref{theorem:thomassen-planar-hypo-minimal}. Zamfirescu showed that it does not hold if $n = \ell p$ and $k = \ell (p - 1) + 1$, where $p \ge 5$ is odd and $\ell \ge 6$, using the construction $G_{p,\ell}$ in Section~\ref{sec:tube-graph}. Observation~\ref{obs:2con-n14} tells us all graphs which are negative examples of RQ\ref{rq2} must have at least 14 vertices, while Observation~\ref{obs:3con-hamiltonian} tells us that graphs on 22 vertices or fewer cannot be 3-connected, and therefore must have connectivity at most 2.

We limit the graphs for RQ\ref{rq2} to 2-connected graphs. This is because one can create a disconnected graph or a graph with connectivity 1 with a $k$-cycle in every vertex-deleted subgraph, for every planar graph with minimum degree at least 4 and circumference $k$. This can be by combining two planar graphs $G$ and $H$ (where $V(G) \cap V(H) = \emptyset$) with circumference $k$ and minimum degree at least 4 into one graph, and for the 1-connected case, join two of their vertices. Figure~\ref{fig:1con-case} shows an example of this where $G$ and $H$ are the same Hamiltonian graph on 6 vertices. The red edge in the figure connects the graph without creating new cycles.

In this thesis, we use computer-assisted graph theory to attempt to solve RQ\ref{rq1}, and to find new results for RQ\ref{rq2}. More concretely, for each RQ, we generate all graphs of certain classes, and check them for the desired properties. We will go more in detail as to what graph generation is, and describe some generators that already exist.

\section{Graph Generators and Databases}
\label{sec:generators}

A graph generator is an algorithm that computes and outputs a list of graphs of a specific class or specific properties. Most generators take in at least one parameter, commonly the order of the graphs to be generated, and output all graphs that are pairwise non-isomorphic, belonging to a specified graph class. By combining an existing graph generator which exhaustively generates all graphs with a certain property (such as 2-connected graphs on 13 nodes), and combining this with a program which checks these graphs for a second property (such as having circumference 11 with an empty 11-cycle intersection), one can prove the existence (or nonexistence) of a graph with both properties without designing and implementing an entirely new graph generator~\cite{article:jorik-jooken}.

Different generators are designed for different graph classes. The \texttt{nauty} package~\cite{lib:nauty} contains many programs and tools for computer-assisted graph theory, including some generators. A widely used general-purpose generator is \texttt{geng}, which generates all pairwise non-isomorphic graphs for a specified order. The number of graphs for this class goes up rapidly for increasing order of the graph, as for 13 nodes there are $50\,502\,031\,367\,952$ generated graphs. That is why adding constraints to generation, or using specialized generators, is preferred. Besides the number of vertices, \texttt{geng} supports restrictions on the number of edges, and minimum and maximum degree. It also allows restricting output to connected or 2-connected graphs, bipartite graphs, 3-cycle-free graphs, and several other graph classes. The \texttt{nauty} package also contains the program \texttt{pickg}, which can take generated graphs as input, and output only those graphs which satisfy additional properties, such as 2-connected graphs~\cite{article:jorik-jooken}~\cite{lib:nauty}.

The specialized generator \texttt{plantri}~\cite{lib:plantri} generates specifically planar graphs. The program directly generates non-isomorphic planar graphs and supports several subclasses, including triangulations, quadrangulations, cubic and quartic planar graphs. Interesting for this thesis, \texttt{plantri} also allows restrictions on parameters such as minimum connectivity, minimum degree and the number of edges. There is one drawback, namely that for general planar graphs, generated graphs are considered non-isomorphic with respect to the embedding on a sphere, which results in some isomorphic graphs to be generated when generating graphs which are 2-connected or 1-connected~\cite{article:jorik-jooken}~\cite{lib:plantri}. 

Similarly, \texttt{snarkhunter} is specialized for cubic graphs, which are graphs where all vertices have 3 neighbors, and snarks, which are cubic graphs which do not admit a proper $3$-edge-coloring. This generator also allows for specifying a lower bound on the girth of the graph~\cite{article:jorik-jooken}.

There also exists the graph generator \texttt{GenerateUHG}, which can generate graph with a set number of distinct Hamilton cycles. This number can be set to 0 to generate non-Hamiltonian graphs. This generator also allows for setting an upper bound for the maximum degree, as well as restricting generation to planar graphs, among other restrictions~\cite{lib:UHG}.

Besides graph generators, there also exist databases of graphs storing a select number of graphs. These databases often also store the invariants of each graph, and allow for searching the database for graphs adhering to (a set of) these invariants. Four examples of graph databases are \textit{DiscreteZOO}, \textit{PHOEG}, \textit{Encyclopedia of Graphs}, and \textit{House of Graphs}~\cite{data:houseofgraphs}~\cite{article:jorik-jooken}.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter has outlined the theoretical background and previous work underlying the research done in this thesis. Starting from the study of hypohamiltonian graphs by Thomassen, with this work being continued to longest cycles through the work of van Aardt et al.\ and Zamfirescu, leading to two remaining open problems. The first concerns the existence of a $2$-connected graph of circumference $11$ whose longest cycles have empty intersection, while the second asks for which values of $k$ the statement in RQ\ref{rq2} on planar graphs of minimal degree 4 holds.

The discussed results also motivate the computational approach adopted in this thesis. Previous work has established graph classes and lower bounds that substantially reduce the search space, with specialized exhaustive graph generation making it feasible to investigate these large classes of candidate graphs. The next chapter describes the graph classes, generators, and algorithms used to address the two research questions.

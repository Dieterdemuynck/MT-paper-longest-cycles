Take a $p,\ell$ tube graph as shown before, with $p$ odd. For simplicity, from
here on out, let any use of a numerical index be an integer modulo $p$, such
that for example $a_0$ = $a_p$. Subdivide all edges $(a_i,a_{i+1})$ and
$(b_i,b_{i+1})$ until each subdivided edge has a total of $d+1$ vertices,
including the original vertices (i.e. forms a path of length $d$). We denote
this graph as $G_{p,\ell,d}$, and denote the path on these vertices from $a_i$
to $a_{i+1}$ by $D_{a,i}$, and similarly the path from $b_i$ to $b_{i+1}$ by
$D_{b,i}$.

We also define the sets $X_i = {a_i,b_i}$, $A_i = {a_i,a_{i+1}}$,
$B_i = {b_i,b_{i+1}}$, as each of these are vertex-cuts of the graph.
%
% State that all three are vertex cuts.
%
% Prove the local consequence:
% every cycle using a_i either
%   (i) continues through both adjacent D_a paths, or
%   (ii) uses exactly one adjacent D_a path together with H_i.
% Do the analogous statement for b_i.
%
% This is the local structural lemma used everywhere later.
Since these sets are vertex-cuts, any cycle going through an internal vertex or
edge of $D_{a,i}$, $D_{b,i}$, or $H_i$ (i.e. traverses these subgraphs) must
also contain $A_i$, $B_i$, resp. $X_i$. Furthermore, the only subgraphs
incident on $a_i$ are $D_{a,i-1}$, $D_{a,i}$, and $H_i$, and thus every cycle
containing $a_i$ must either continue through both adjacent $D_{a,i-1}$ and
$D_{a,i}$ subgraphs, or through exactly one of the aforementioned subgraphs and
$H_i$. The only exceptions are cycles contained entirely within one of these
subgraphs, which cannot be longest cycles. An analogous reasoning can be used
for cycles going through $b_i$.
%
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

We now continue to show that there are only two candidate longest cycles, by
first showing that a longest cycle must traverse an $H_i$, and then showing
there are two types of cycles containing this $H_i$.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
% P3 (Main structural lemma)
%
% Show that once a cycle traverses some H_i,

\paragraph{$H_i$ requirement}
If we were to remove all internal nodes and edges belonging to $H_i$, we get
two disjoint cycles. As such, the longest cycles which do not traverse any
$H_i$ are contained within the two subgraphs

$$
\bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_p}D_{a,i},\;
\bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_p}D_{b,i}
$$

These cannot be the longest cycles of the graph, as one can create a longer
cycle by picking any $i$, removing the internal nodes of $D_{a,i}$ resp.
$D_{b,i}$, and creating a new cycle through $H_i$, $D_{b,i}$ resp. $D_{a,i}$,
and $H_{i+1}$. The original cycle has length $pd$, while this new cycle has
length $pd+2l$. As a result, this new cycle is larger than the former cycle,
since by construction $\ell > 0$. As a result, any longest cycle must contain
at least one $H_i$.

% Define:

If a cycle traverses an $H_i$, it must enter at $a_i$ from either
$D_{a,i}$ or $D_{a,i-1}$, and leave at $b_i$ through either $D_{b,i}$ or
$D_{b,i-1}$. We now show there are two types of cycles these choices create.

\paragraph{Same-direction cycles}
A cycle can enter and leave an $H_i$ in the same direction, either through
$D_{a,i-1}$ and $D_{b,i-1}$ or through $D_{a,i}$ and $D_{b,i}$. For simplicity,
assume a cycle $C$ traverses $H_0$, $D_{a,0}$, and $D_{b,0}$. At $a_1$, there
are two possibilities: to either traverse $H_1$, closing the cycle, or to
traverse $D_{a,1}$. The latter option disallows any traversal of $H_1$, and
therefore forces the choice at $b_i$ to traverse $D_{b,1}$. We then again have
the same options for traversal at $a_2$. One can see this choice of traversing
$D_{a,i}$ and $D_{b,i}$ instead of $H_i$ as extending the otherwise closed
cycle with $2d$ vertices.

Repeating this argument successively for $a_2$, $a_3$, \dots, $a_{p-1}$ yields
the longest same-direction cycle through $H_0$, $D_{a,0}$, and $D_{b,0}$ with
length $(p-1)(2d)+2\ell$, and traverses all vertices

$$
\bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_p \setminus \{p-1\}}V(D_{a,i})
\;\cup\;
\bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_p \setminus \{p-1\}}V(D_{b,i})
\;\cup\; 
V(H_0)
\;\cup\; 
V(H_{p-1})
$$

The reasoning for $D_{a,-1}$ and $D_{b,-1}$, and for other $H_i$'s, is
analogous. Note that all vertices $a_i$ and $b_i$ meet all these cycles.

\paragraph{Opposite-direction cycles}
The only other option for a cycle traversing $H_i$ is to enter and leave
$H_i$ in opposite directions, either through $D_{a,i-1}$ and $D_{b,i}$ or
through $D_{a,i}$ and $D_{b,i-1}$.

Say a cycle traversed $H_0$ and $D_{a,0}$. There are again two possibilities at
$a_1$: to traverse $D_{a,1}$, or to traverse $H_1$. In the latter option, the
traversal of $D_{b,1}$ is forced, as the only other option to traverse
$D_{b,0}$ would result in the same-side case from before. From then on, this
option repeats: at any endpoint $b_i$, either $D_{b,i}$ is traversed, or $H_i$
and $D_{a,i}$ are traversed, and analogous for endpoints $a_i$, up until
$i=p-1$. There, $D_{b,p-1}$ must be traversed to complete the cycle, either
from $a_{p-1}$ through $H_{p-1}$, or directly from $b_{p-1}$. In total, every
$H_i$ traversed this way adds $d+\ell$ vertices to the cycle (including
$H_0$, where $b_0$ is counted from the traversal of $D_{b,p-1}$), while every
$H_i$ avoided adds only $d$ vertices.

By construction, a cycle in $G$ cannot traverse all $H_i$. Since $X_i$ is a
vertex-cut in $G$, the subgraph $H_i$ can only be traversed by entering through
$a_i$ and exiting through $b_i$. As a result, a cycle must visit an even number
of $H_i$'s. Since $p$ is odd, at least one $H_i$ must be avoided. Thus, the
largest opposite-side cycle must traverse (1) a maximal amount of $H_i$'s, and (2) at most all but one $H_i$. We can construct a cycle $C$ avoiding exactly one $H_i$, with length $(p-1)(d+\ell) + d$. Put

\paragraph{Length comparison and conclusion}
We've determined there are two types of cycles with the potential of being
longest cycles, the length of which are $(p-1)(2d)+2\ell$ for same-direction
cycles and $(p-1)(d+\ell)+d$ for opposite-direction cycles. Only in the
latter case can we construct a cycle for every vertex avoiding that vertex. As
a result, there exists a 2-connected, planar graph $G$ with circumference $(p-1)(d+\ell)+d$ for which no vertex
meets every longest cycle if

\begin{align*}
{}      && (p-1)(2d)+2\ell
        &\le (p-1)(d+\ell)+d\\
\iff\;  && d(p-2)
        &\le \ell(p-3).
\end{align*}

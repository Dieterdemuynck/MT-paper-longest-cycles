\chapter{Results for RQ1}
\label{cha:results}

In this chapter, we discuss the correctness tests performed and their results on the algorithm designed for research question \ref{rq1}, as well as the results of running the three different pipelines from Section~\ref{sec:generators-used} and the observations gained from them. We also shortly discuss the time each pipeline took for each of the parameters, and how these influenced the choice of which pipeline to use to generate graphs of larger orders or maximum degrees. Lastly, based on the results found, we state and proof a new theorem expanding on the results found in Section~\ref{sec:modified-petersen}.

\section{Correctness tests for Algorithm~\ref{alg:find-k-intersection}}
\label{sec:1-correct}

To ensure the designed algorithm works as intended, and is implemented correctly, several correctness tests were performed. We have tested the algorithm using multiple different (lists of) known graphs as input. These graphs include a list of all cycles on 3 vertices up to 30 vertices, the Petersen graph, the modified Petersen graph from Section~\ref{sec:modified-petersen}, some graphs of the form $G_{p,\ell}$ from Section~\ref{sec:tube-graph}, and lists of graphs of a set circumference obtained from House of Graphs~\cite{data:houseofgraphs}. Using a configuration file, we can also slightly adapt the implementation to output all graphs, allowing us to see the intersection size detected of graphs that normally have a $k$-cycle intersection of more than one vertex. All \texttt{graph6} strings for graphs mentioned were created using the graph drawing function of House of Graphs~\cite{data:houseofgraphs}.

For the following, we enable the program to output all graphs of circumference $k$. A list of individual cycles on 3 vertices up to 30 vertices was given as input to the program, which was configured to output all graphs of circumference $k$. We test the program on this list for $k\in\{3,10,11,28,30\}$. The program correctly identifies the number of graphs with circumference smaller than the parameter $k$ (0, 7, 8, 25, and 27 respectively), it identifies and outputs the cycle of length $k$ to have circumference $k$ and a $k$-cycle intersection of all $k$ vertices, and the program also outputs the correct number of graphs larger than $k$ (27, 20, 19, 2, and 0). For $k=30$, we input the \texttt{graph6} string from the output \texttt{CSV} file into House of Graphs'~\cite{data:houseofgraphs} graph drawing function, and confirm that this is the expected cycle on 30 vertices.

Since the Petersen graph is a hypohamiltonian graph of order 10~\cite{article:van-aardt}, we expect the program (with $k$ set to 9) to correctly identify this graph as a graph of circumference 9 with an empty 9-cycle intersection, marking it as a ``solution'' in the output \texttt{CSV} file. This happens as expected, and does not happen when $k$ is set to 8 or 10, again as expected.

The construction by C.\ T.\ Zamfirescu starting from the Petersen graph, by inserting a vertex on each of the three edges incident to one vertex $v$, has order 13 and circumference 11, with only one vertex $v$ in the 11-cycle intersection. For $k$ set to 11, the program correctly outputs the graph marked as a ``single-vertex intersection'', and has the correct intersection of one vertex, $v$.

The other construction by C.\ T.\ Zamfirescu of the form $G_{p,\ell}$ has, for $p\ge4$ and $\ell\ge2$, order $p(\ell+1)$ and circumference $(p-1)(\ell+1)+1$, and an empty longest-cycle intersection. For $G_\{4,2\}$, $G_\{5,2\}$, and $G_\{7,3\}$, this graph has circumference 10, 13, and 25 respectively. The program correctly identifies the graph as having an empty $k$-cycle intersection with circumference $k$ for each respective graph and circumference. Since we know from Section~\ref{sec:tube-graph} that arbitrary edges can be added within each individual $H_i$, we also test these graphs where each $H_i$ subgraph is a complete graph (that is, a graph in which every two distinct vertices are adjacent). The result remains the same, as expected.

Lastly, three lists of graphs were downloaded from House of Graphs~\cite{data:houseofgraphs} for testing. These are a list of 2-connected graphs on at most 32 vertices with circumference $c$, with $c=10$ (533 graphs), $c=11$ (265 graphs), and $c=12$ (495 graphs). Setting $k=11$ in the program should result in all graphs being considered less than $k$ for the first list, equal to $k$ for the second, and larger than $k$ for the third. This is exactly what we observe.

These tests give adequate confirmation that Algorithm~\ref{alg:find-k-intersection} was correctly designed and implemented, and functions as intended.

\section{Computational Results}

In Section~\ref{sec:generators-used}, we discussed the generation pipelines used to investigate the first research question. Combining these with our own program which implements Algorithm~\ref{alg:find-k-intersection}, these pipelines are thus as follows.
\begin{verbatim}
  generateUHG <n> H0 D<d> | pickg -c2 | algorithm1
  geng -CD<d> <n> | algorithm1
  plantri <n> -gpc2 | algorithm1
\end{verbatim}
Since the pipeline using \texttt{generateUHG} and the pipeline using \texttt{geng} generate graph classes from which we can draw the same conclusions, we continue to the time all pipelines took for certain input values, such that we can compare these two pipelines and choose one pipeline to continue with our computations.

\subsection{Results and observations for RQ\ref{rq1}}

As shown in the previous sections, we generated and checked all 2-connected graphs on 13 vertices with maximum degree at most 6, on 14 vertices with maximum degree at most 5, on 15 vertices with maximum degree at most 4, and on 16 to 22 vertices with maximum degree at most 3. For each of these, the number of graphs which have circumference 11 can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:A}, as well as the number of graphs per the number of vertices in the intersection of all 11-cycles. This brings us to the following observation.
\begin{observation}
  All 2-connected graphs with circumference 11 on at most 22 vertices with maximum degree at most 3 have at least one vertex meeting every 11-cycle.
\end{observation}
We also observe that the same is true for graphs on at most 15 vertices with maximum degree at most 4, on at most 14 vertices with maximum degree at most 5, and on at most 13 vertices with maximum degree at most 6.

Additionally, we also generated and checked all 2-connected planar graphs of 13 vertices. The number of planar graphs generated with circumference 11 and for these the number of graphs per number of vertices in the intersection of all 11-cycles can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:plantri}. These intersections bring us to the following observation.
\begin{observation}
  All 2-connected planar graphs with circumference 11 on 13 vertices have at least four vertices meeting every 11-cycle.
\end{observation}

\subsection{Graphs with intersection 1}

The smallest intersection found in any generated graph during execution had an intersection of one vertex. While these graphs are not solutions directly, they may give an insight to a potential solution. We already know of a graph on 13 vertices $P$ with an 11-cycle intersection of 1. This graph has a maximum degree of 3, as shown in Section~\ref{sec:modified-petersen}. During generation, only one graph was found with an 11-cycle intersection of 1. We expect this graph to be isomorphic to $P$, and a diagram of this result as in Figure~\ref{fig:d3n13i1} confirms this.

Increasing the upper bound for the maximum degree to 4 gives two new graphs with intersection 1 distinct from the known graph with a single vertex intersection, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:d4n13i1}. Increasing this upper bound to 5 gives six new graphs, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:d5n13i1}. The figures show that the new graphs on 13 nodes are the graph $P$ with one or more additional edges, all incident on the vertex $v$.

Additionally, increasing the order of the graph to 14 at maximum degree 4 gives us one new graph with intersection 1, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:d4n14i1}. This figure shows that this is the graph $P$ with a duplicated edge with an inserted vertex, i.e. the path $(v, x_1', w_1)$ was inserted into the graph.

These observations led us to assume there exists a 2-connected graph with circumference 11 of any order greater than or equal to 13, with only one vertex in the intersection of all 11-cycles. We formalize this as following theorem, which we also immediately prove.

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem:single-vertex-intersection}
For every integer $n \ge 13$ and every $d \in\{n-10,\ldots,n-1\}$, there exists a $2$-connected graph $G$ of order $n$, circumference $11$, maximum degree $\Delta(G) = d$, and minimum degree $\delta(G) = 2$, such that exactly one vertex meets every longest cycle.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Take the graph $P$ from Section~\ref{sec:modified-petersen}, where $x_1$, $x_2$, and $x_3$ are the three vertices of degree 2, incident on the common vertex $v$, and the vertices $w_1$, $w_2$, and $w_3$ respectively. Set 
$$
Y = V(P) \setminus \{v, x_1, x_2, x_3\}.
$$
$P$ has order 13 and circumference 11, with one vertex $v$ meeting every 11-cycle, as shown in~\cite{article:zamfirescu}. We now show that we can add any edge $(v,y)$ with $y \in Y$ to $P$, and (subject to that condition) add any number of vertices $x_{i,j}$ adjacent only to $v$ and $w_i$, and the resulting graph $G$ will remain 2-connected, with circumference 11, and with $v$ the only vertex in the intersection of all 11-cycles.

Additionally, since adding edges also does not remove any existing cycles, all $11$-cycles of $P$ remain present in the resulting graph, and hence $v$ is still contained in every original $11$-cycle.

To conclude that $v$ lies in every $11$-cycle of the final graph, it therefore suffices to show that no new $11$-cycle avoiding $v$ is created. In addition, to ensure that the circumference remains $11$, it is enough to prove that no cycle of length greater than $11$ is introduced by the added edges and vertices. Finally, we must also verify that the graph remains $2$-connected after addition of the paths $(v, x_{i,j}, w_i)$.

\paragraph{Adding an edge $(v,y)$}
We proceed by induction on the number of edges of the form $(v,y)$ with $y\in Y$ that have been added. Suppose $G'$ is a graph constructed by adding $0 \le k < |Y|$ distinct edges $(v,y')$ for $y' \in Y$ to $P$. Also assume that $G'$ is 2-connected with circumference 11 and an 11-cycle intersection equal to ${v}$. Now consider adding the new edge where $(v,y)\notin E(G')$ for $y \in Y$, creating a new graph $G''$.

First, consider a cycle $C$ in $G''$ containing the newly added edge $(v,y)$. Since $C$ contains $(v,y)$, it can use at most one further edge incident with $v$. In particular, $C$ can contain at most one of the three vertices $x_1,x_2,x_3$. This is the case because, for each $i\in\{1,2,3\}$, $\{v,w_i\}$ is a vertex-cut separating $x_i$ from the rest of the graph. Thus, any cycle containing $x_i$ must enter through $v$ and leave through $w_i$. A cycle containing $(v,y)$ can contain at most one of the three paths
\[
(v,x_1,w_1),\qquad (v,x_2,w_2),\qquad (v,x_3,w_3).
\]
Consequently, it follows that $C$ avoids at least two of the three vertices $x_1,x_2,x_3$. Because $G''$ has 13 vertices, the length of $C$ can therefore be at most 11.

Secondly, by definition, $C$ must contain the vertex $v$. Additionally, any cycle $C'$ not containing the edge $(v,y)$ is also a cycle of the graph $G'$, where we know the intersection of all 11-cycles is $\{v\}$. Thus, the intersection of all 11-cycles in $G''$ remains $\{v\}$, and the circumference of $G''$ is 11. Lastly, adding edges does not remove any existing paths, so by definition, the graph $G''$ is also 2-connected.

We know that $P$ is a 2-connected graph of circumference 11, where the 11-cycle intersection equals $\{v\}$. By induction, we've shown that one can add any number $\le 9$ of distinct edges $(v,y)$ with $y \in Y$ to $P$ such that the resulting graph $P'$ is 2-connected, has circumference 11, and has $v$ as the only vertex in the intersection of all 11-cycles.

\paragraph{Adding a path $(v, x_{i,j}, w_i)$}
Take any graph $P'$ obtained from $P$ by adding any number of distinct
edges $(v,y)$ with $y \in Y$. We again use induction on the total number $j$ of additional vertices $x_{i,j}$, assuming that any graph $P'$ with less than $j$ additional paths $(v, x_{i,j}, w_i)$ has already been established to be 2-connected, have circumference 11, and an 11-cycle intersection equal to $\{v\}$. Choose any graph $G'$ with $j-1$ of these additional paths, and choose any $i\in\{1,2,3\}$. Now consider the addition of $x_{i,j}$, resulting in the graph $G''$.

To show that the resulting graph is $2$-connected, first consider the vertex-deleted subgraph $G'' \setminus \{x_i\}$ and denote it as $G'''$. Observe that this graph is isomorphic to $G'$, as one can map $x_{i,j}$ and its incident edges onto $x_i$ and the respective edges, and map every other vertex and edge onto itself. By the induction hypothesis, $G'$ is $2$-connected and therefore $G'''$ is too. Thus, by definition, for every vertex $u\in V(G'')\setminus\{x_i,x_{i,j}\}$, there are two internally vertex-disjoint paths from $x_{i,j}$ to $u$ in $G''\setminus\{x_i\}$, and hence in $G''$. Trivially, one can construct two internally vertex-disjoint paths from $x_{i,j}$ to $x_i$ in $G''$, namely $(x_{i,j},v,x_i)$ and $(x_{i,j},w_i,x_i)$. Since $G'$ is 2-connected, we have that $G''$ must also be 2-connected.

For the circumference, observe that $\{v,w_i\}$ is a vertex-cut separating the vertices $x_i$ and $x_{i,j}$ from the rest of the graph. Therefore, any cycle containing $x_i$ or $x_{i,j}$ must pass through the vertices $v$ and $w_i$. Since these are the only vertices adjacent to the vertex $x_i$ and similarly to $x_{i,j}$, we have that the only cycle containing both is the cycle
\[
(v,x_i,w_i,x_{i,j},v)
\]
which has length $4$, and consequently not a longest cycle of the graph $G''$. Any cycle going through $x_{i,j}$ and not $x_i$ is entirely contained within $G'''$, which we know is isomorphic to $G'$, and therefore by the induction hypothesis has length at most 11 and must go through the vertex $v$. Any cycle which includes only $x_i$ or neither $x_i$ nor $x_{i,j}$ is entirely contained within $G'$, and by the induction hypothesis has at most length 11, with the intersection of all such 11-cycle equaling $\{v\}$. In conclusion, the graph $G''$ has circumference 11, and the intersection of all 11-cycles is $\{v\}$.

We know that $P'$ is a 2-connected graph, with circumference 11, with an 11-cycle intersection equal to $\{v\}$. By induction, we have proven that adding any number $j$ of paths $(v,x_{i,j},w_i)$ to $P'$ results in a 2-connected graph with circumference 11, with an 11-cycle intersection equal to $\{v\}$. Since $P'$ was chosen at random, this counts for all graphs $P'$ which are the result of adding any number of distinct edges $(v,y)$ for $y \in Y$ to the graph $P$.

\paragraph{Conclusion}
Starting with $P$, we may add any number of vertices $x_{i,j}$ for each $i\in\{1,2,3\}$, and any number of edges $(v,y)$ with $y\in Y$, while preserving $2$-connectivity, circumference 11, and the 11-cycle intersection $\{v\}$. Observe that in $P$, the maximum degree is 3, and $v$ has a degree of 3. Also observe that every addition of an edge or path as before increases the degree of $v$ and one other vertex by exactly 1. All vertices $x_i$ and the inserted vertices $x_{i,j}$ all have degree 2. As a result, $v$ must have the maximum degree of all vertices. There are 9 vertices in $Y$, and adding a path as before increases the order of the graph by 1. Thus, we conclude that a graph $G$ can be constructed with order $r + 13$ for all $r \ge 0$, and maximum degree $\Delta(G) = n-10+k$ for all $k \in \{0,\dots,9\}$, with minimum degree 2, by adding $r$ distinct paths $(v,x_{i,j},w_i)$ and adding $k$ distinct edges $(v,y)$ with $y \in Y$, to $P$, where $G$ is 2-connected, has circumference 11, and has an 11-cycle intersection $\{v\}$.

\end{proof}

\section{Conclusion}

In this Chapter, we conclude that there is no 2-connected graph of circumference 11 with no vertex meeting every 11-cycle for graphs of order at most $n$ and maximum degree at most $d$ for the pairs $(n,d)\in\{(13,6),(14,5),(15,4),(22,3)\}$, and that all 2-connected planar graph of circumference 11 and order 13 must have at least 4 vertices meeting every cycle. We also find that there is a 2-connected graph of circumference 11 of any order greater than or equal to 13, for which the intersection of all 11-cycles is only one vertex.

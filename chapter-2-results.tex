\section{New Values for RQ2}

Stuyding the gadget mentioned in section~\ref{sec:gadget} led to an insight in finding new negative examples to RQ\ref{rq2}. By expanding not only the inner edges between the cycles, but also expanding the edges of the cycles to a new length, graphs could be created with longest cycle that differ in length compared to the order of the graph than those already known. Similar to graphs of the form $G_{p,\ell}$, these graphs also have no vertex meeting every cycle.

We will now prove that, for $\ell \ge 6$, $d \ge 5$, and $p \ge 3$ integers with $p$ is odd, where $d(p-2) \le \ell(p-3)$, there exists a 2-connected planar graph with with minimal degree 4, order~$p(\ell+2d-1)$ and circumference~$(p-1)(d+\ell)+d$, whose intersection of all longest cycles is empty. The proof takes inspiration from the proof of the similar Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 in~\cite{article:zamfirescu}.

\subsection{Proof}

Take the graph $G_{p,\ell}$ from Section \ref{sec:tube-graph}, with $p$ odd. For simplicity, from
here on out, let any use of a numerical index be an integer modulo $p$, such
that for example $a_0 = a_p$. Insert vertices on each of the edges
$(a_i,a_{i+1})$ and $(b_i,b_{i+1})$ until each is a path of length $d$, i.e.
has a total of $d+1$ vertices, including the original vertices. We denote this
graph as $G_{p,\ell,d}$, and denote the path on these vertices from $a_i$ to
$a_{i+1}$ by $D_{a,i}$, and similarly the path from $b_i$ to $b_{i+1}$ by
$D_{b,i}$.
Figure~\ref{fig:g532} shows an example graph $G_{5,3,2}$.

We also define the sets $X_i = {a_i,b_i}$, $A_i = {a_i,a_{i+1}}$,
$B_i = {b_i,b_{i+1}}$, as each of these are vertex-cuts of the graph.
Since these sets are vertex-cuts, any cycle going through an internal vertex or
edge of $D_{a,i}$, $D_{b,i}$, or $H_i$ (i.e. traverses these subgraphs) must
also contain $A_i$, $B_i$, resp. $X_i$. Furthermore, the only subgraphs
incident on $a_i$ are $D_{a,i-1}$, $D_{a,i}$, and $H_i$, and thus every cycle
containing $a_i$ must either continue through both adjacent $D_{a,i-1}$ and
$D_{a,i}$ subgraphs, or through exactly one of the aforementioned subgraphs and
$H_i$. The only exceptions are cycles contained entirely within one of these
subgraphs, which cannot be longest cycles. An analogous reasoning can be used
for cycles going through $b_i$.

We now continue to show that there are only two candidate longest cycles, by
first showing that a longest cycle must traverse an $H_i$, and then showing
there are two types of cycles containing this $H_i$.

\paragraph{$H_i$ requirement}
If we were to remove all internal vertices and edges belonging to $H_i$, we get
two disjoint cycles. As such, the longest cycles which do not traverse any
$H_i$ are contained within the two subgraphs

$$
\bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_p}D_{a,i}\; \text{and} \;
\bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_p}D_{b,i}
$$

These cannot be the longest cycles of the graph, as one can create a longer
cycle by picking any $i$, removing the internal vertices of $D_{a,i}$ resp.
$D_{b,i}$, and creating a new cycle through $H_i$, $D_{b,i}$ resp. $D_{a,i}$,
and $H_{i+1}$. The original cycle has length $pd$, while this new cycle has
length $pd+2l$. As a result, this new cycle is larger than the former cycle,
since by construction $\ell > 0$. As a result, any longest cycle must contain
at least one $H_i$.

If a cycle traverses an $H_i$, it must enter at $a_i$ from either
$D_{a,i}$ or $D_{a,i-1}$, and leave at $b_i$ through either $D_{b,i}$ or
$D_{b,i-1}$. We now show there are two types of cycles these choices create.

\paragraph{Same-direction cycles}
A cycle can enter and leave an $H_i$ in the same direction, either through
$D_{a,i-1}$ and $D_{b,i-1}$ or through $D_{a,i}$ and $D_{b,i}$. For simplicity,
assume a cycle $C$ traverses $H_0$, $D_{a,0}$, and $D_{b,0}$. At $a_1$, there
are two possibilities: to either traverse $H_1$, closing the cycle as in Figure
\ref{fig:same-side:closed}, or to traverse $D_{a,1}$. The latter option
disallows any traversal of $H_1$, and therefore forces the choice at $b_i$ to
traverse $D_{b,1}$, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:same-side:continued}. We then
again have the same options for traversal at $a_2$. One can see this choice of
traversing $D_{a,i}$ and $D_{b,i}$ instead of $H_i$ as extending the otherwise
closed cycle with $2d$ vertices.
Repeating this argument successively for $a_2$, $a_3$, \dots, $a_{p-1}$ yields
the longest same-direction cycle through $H_0$, $D_{a,0}$, and $D_{b,0}$ with
length $(p-1)(2d)+2\ell$, and traverses all vertices

$$
\bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_p \setminus \{p-1\}}V(D_{a,i})
\;\cup\;
\bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_p \setminus \{p-1\}}V(D_{b,i})
\;\cup\; 
V(H_0)
\;\cup\; 
V(H_{p-1})
$$

The reasoning for $D_{a,-1}$ and $D_{b,-1}$, and for other $H_i$'s, is
analogous. Note that all vertices $a_i$ and $b_i$ meet all these cycles.

\paragraph{Opposite-direction cycles}
The only other option for a cycle traversing $H_i$ is to enter and leave
$H_i$ in opposite directions, either through $D_{a,i-1}$ and $D_{b,i}$ or
through $D_{a,i}$ and $D_{b,i-1}$.

Say a cycle traversed $H_0$ and $D_{a,0}$. There are again two possibilities at
$a_1$: to traverse $D_{a,1}$, or to traverse $H_1$. In the latter option, the
traversal of $D_{b,1}$ is forced, as the only other option to traverse
$D_{b,0}$ would result in the same-side case from before. From then on, this
option repeats: at any endpoint $b_i$, either $D_{b,i}$ is traversed, or $H_i$
and $D_{a,i}$ are traversed, and analogous for endpoints $a_i$, up until
$i=p-1$. There, $D_{b,p-1}$ must be traversed to complete the cycle, either
from $a_{p-1}$ through $H_{p-1}$, or directly from $b_{p-1}$. In total, every
$H_i$ traversed this way adds $d+\ell$ vertices to the cycle (including
$H_0$, where $b_0$ is counted from the traversal of $D_{b,p-1}$), while every
$H_i$ avoided adds only $d$ vertices.

By construction, a cycle in $G$ cannot traverse all $H_i$. Since $X_i$ is a
vertex-cut in $G$, the subgraph $H_i$ can only be traversed by entering through
$a_i$ and exiting through $b_i$. As a result, a cycle must visit an even number
of $H_i$'s. Since $p$ is odd, at least one $H_i$ must be avoided. Thus, the
largest opposite-side cycle must traverse (1) a maximal amount of $H_i$'s, and (2) at most all but one $H_i$. We can construct a cycle $C$ avoiding exactly one $H_i$, with length $(p-1)(d+\ell) + d$. Put

$$
C=
\left(
\bigcup_{i\neq0}H_i
\right)
\cup
\left(
\bigcup_{j\ \verb|even|}D_{b,j}
\right)
\cup
\left(
\bigcup_{k\ \verb|odd|}D_{a,k}
\right).
$$

where $C$ omits all vertices $V(H_0)\setminus\{b_0\}$ and
$V(D_{a,0})\setminus\{a_1\}$. Figure~\ref{fig:opposite-side:g532} shows this cycle for a graph $G_{5,3,2}$. For all other vertices, a cycle of length
$(p-1)(d+\ell)+d$ can be constructed in the same way.

\paragraph{Length comparison}
We've determined there are two types of cycles with the potential of being
longest cycles, the length of which are $(p-1)(2d)+2\ell$ for same-direction
cycles and $(p-1)(d+\ell)+d$ for opposite-direction cycles. Only in the
latter case can we construct a cycle for every vertex avoiding that vertex. As
a result, there exists a 2-connected, planar graph $G$ with circumference
$(p-1)(d+\ell)+d$ for which no vertex meets every longest cycle if

\[
\begin{aligned}
(p-1)(2d)+2\ell
    &\le (p-1)(d+\ell)+d\\
\iff\qquad
d(p-2)
    &\le \ell(p-3).
\end{aligned}
\]

\paragraph{Minimal degree 4}
Let $G$ be a graph $G_{p,\ell,d}$ with $p$ odd, $\ell \ge 5$, and $d \ge 5$ with $d(p-2) \le \ell(p-3)$. Similar to the proof of Proposition 1 in~\cite{article:zamfirescu}, observe that that in each $D_{a,i}$, $D_{b,i}$, and $H_i$, we may add edges as desired within each respective subgraph (but without adding edges between internal nodes of different subgraphs) without altering the fact that the intersection of all longest cycles remains empty. We denote these modified paths with additional edges as $D_{a,i}'$, $D_{b,i}'$, respectively $H_i'$.

Trivially, if we choose $D_{a,i}'$ and $D_{b,i}'$ to each be the path $P_d^2$, i.e. a path of length $d$ with an additional edge between all vertices at distance 2 as in Figure~\ref{fig:path-2}, and similarly choose $H_i'$ to be the path $P_\ell^2$, then $G$ remains planar. Observe that, for each of these subgrahs, all internal vertices except two have degree 4, and two internal vertices, say $w$ and $w'$, have degree 3. These vertices $w$ and $w'$ are adjacent to an endpoint of the (modified) paths, say $x$ and $y$. Adding the edges $wx$ and $w'y$ when $d$ (resp. $\ell$) is odd as in Figure~\ref{fig:degree-4-opposite-side}, and adding the edge $ww'$ when $d$ (resp. $\ell$) is even as in Figure~\ref{fig:degree-4-same-side}, provides a minimal degree 4 for the graph $G$ when $\ell,d \ge 5$.

\paragraph{Conclusion}
As a result, the previous construction shows that for all integers $p\ge5$ odd, $\ell \ge 6$, $d \ge 5$, where $d(p-2) \le \ell(p-3)$, there exists a planar graph with minimal degree 4 of order~$p(\ell+2d-1)$ and circumference~$(p-1)(d+\ell)+d$, where the intersection of all longest cycles is empty.

\hfill $\square$ \\

\subsection{Graphs with intersection 1}

The smallest intersection found in any generated graph during execution had an intersection of one vertex. While these graphs are not solutions directly, they may give an insight to a potential solution. We already know of a graph $P$ with an intersection of 1 of order 13 with maximal degree 3, as shown in Section~\ref{sec:modified-petersen}, which we expect to be isomorphic to the only intersection 1 result found in the graphs of order 13 with maximal degree 3. A diagram of this result as in Figure~\ref{fig:d3n13i1} confirms this.

Increasing the upper bound for the maximal degree to 4 gives two new graphs with intersection 1 distinct from the known graph with a single vertex intersection, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:d4n13i1}. Increasing this upper bound to 5 gives six new graphs, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:d5n13i1}. The figures show that the new graphs on 13 nodes are the graph $P$ with one or more additional edges, all incident on the vertex $v$.

Additionally, increasing the order of the graph to 14 at maximal degree 4 gives us one new graph with intersection 1, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:d4n14i1}. This figure shows that this is the graph $P$ with a duplicated subdivided edge, i.e. the path $(v, x_1', w_1)$ was inserted into the graph.

\begin{theorem}
For every integer $n \ge 13$, there exists a $2$-connected graph of order $n$, circumference $11$, and maximum degree $\Delta\in\{n-10,\ldots,n-1\}$ such that exactly one vertex meets every longest cycle.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Take the graph $P$ from Section~\ref{sec:modified-petersen}, where $x_1$, $x_2$, and $x_3$ are the three vertices of degree 2, incident on the common vertex $v$, and the vertices $w_1$, $w_2$, and $w_3$ respectively. Set $Y = V(P) \setminus \{v, x_1, x_2, x_3\}$. $P$ has order 13 and circumference 11, with one vertex meeting every (longest) 11-cycle. We now show that we can add any vertex $x_{i,j}$ adjacent only to $v$ and $w_i$, and add any edge $(v,y)$ with $y \in Y$ to $P$, without changing its circumference or 11-cycle intersection.

Replacing an edge $vw_i$ by several internally disjoint length-$2$ paths
does not create new longest cycles. Indeed, every cycle using one path
$vx_{i,a}w_i$ has an equivalent cycle obtained by replacing this path by
$vx_{i,b}w_i$ for any other subdivision vertex on the same edge.
Consequently, the only genuinely new cycles created are $4$-cycles of
the form
\[
v-x_{i,a}-w_i-x_{i,b}-v.
\]

Similarly, adding edges incident with $v$ cannot create cycles longer
than $11$, since any cycle using such an edge can traverse at most one
subdivided edge and therefore omits at least two subdivision vertices.

Hence, the graph has circumference $11$. Furthermore, every $11$-cycle is
obtained from a longest cycle of the modified Petersen graph by choosing
one representative subdivision vertex on each subdivided edge. In
particular, every longest cycle contains $v$.

Finally, the degree of $v$ equals
\[
k_1+k_2+k_3+t,
\]
where $t\in\{0,\ldots,9\}$ is the number of additional edges incident
with $v$. Since
\[
k_1+k_2+k_3=n-10,
\]
we obtain
\[
\Delta(v)=n-10+t\in\{n-10,\ldots,n-1\},
\]
proving the claim.
\end{proof}
